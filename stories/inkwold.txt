## shop
[art: map-shop | Vell & Apprentice, Maps and Charts.]
The bell over the door of Orrin Vell's map shop rings at eight o'clock every morning, when you unlock it, and at six o'clock every evening, when you lock it again. In between, it rings for sea captains wanting charts, and farmers wanting their boundaries settled, and once, memorably, for a duke who wanted a map of a country he intended to invent. Orrin drew it for him. It was very beautiful. The duke never paid.

You have worked for Orrin Vell for eleven years, since you were small enough to sit on the counter, and in all that time you have never once known him to be late, or early, or anything but exactly where he said he would be. He is a creature of habit the way a river is a creature of habit. Up at six. Porridge with salt, never sugar. The shop at eight. A pipe at eleven, which he lets go out without lighting. Lunch at one, bread and cheese and an apple, eaten at the drawing table with a cloth over the work so he won't drop crumbs on it. Tea at four. Home at six, down the hill to the little house by the bridge, with his coat over his arm if the weather's fine.

Yesterday, he broke every one of those habits.

He didn't eat his lunch. You found the bread and cheese still in the paper at four o'clock, and the apple with one bite out of it. He didn't let you near the drawing table at all; every time you came close, he threw the cloth over it and sent you off to grind pigments, which you'd already ground, or sweep the floor, which you'd already swept. Twice you caught him standing at the big window, not looking at anything, just standing. Once, when he thought you weren't watching, he took the good pen out of its velvet case and held it up to the light and turned it, slowly, the way you'd look at something you were about to give away.

And at six o'clock, when you'd put your coat on and were standing at the door with your hand on the latch, he said your name.

He never says your name. He calls you *apprentice*, or *you*, or, when you've done something particularly stupid, *young person*. But he said your name, and when you turned round he was standing behind the counter with his hands flat on it, as if he needed it to hold him up.

"You've a good hand," he said. "I've never told you that. I should have. You've a better hand than I had at your age. Don't let anyone tell you otherwise. Including me."

You didn't know what to say. You said, "Thank you, Master Vell," and he nodded, and looked at the counter, and said, "Go on home, then. Mind the step."

You thought about it all the way down the hill. You thought about it while you ate your supper, and while you lay in bed listening to the river. You told yourself he was getting old, that was all, that old men got soft in the evenings. You told yourself you'd ask him about it in the morning.

This morning, the bell doesn't ring at all, because the door is already unlocked, and Orrin is not inside.

You stand in the doorway with the key in your hand and look at the shop. Everything is where it always is: the long oak counter, the cabinets of thin drawers where the maps sleep flat, the great globe in its brass cradle, the smell of ink and paper and pipe smoke. Orrin's coat is gone from the peg. His pipe is gone from the dish. His spectacles are lying on the counter, folded, as if he set them down on purpose.

Orrin never goes anywhere without his spectacles. He can't see his own hand without them. You pick them up and slip them into your pocket, without quite knowing why.

"Master Vell?" you call. The shop swallows your voice.

Then you notice the drawing table.

It sits at the back of the shop by the big window, where the light is best, and Orrin has been working at it for the past three weeks on something he wouldn't let you see. Every time you came near, he'd throw a cloth over it and send you off to grind pigments or sweep the floor. The cloth is on the floor now. And on the table, pinned flat at the corners, is a map.

It's a map of nowhere you've ever seen. Hills, forests, a winding river, a city drawn in such fine lines it seems to glitter. In the far corner, on a mountain, a great domed building labelled in Orrin's crabbed hand: AMMERHOLD. THE LIBRARY.

And as you watch, the map moves.

A line of ink crawls out from the edge of the paper, all by itself, as if an invisible pen were drawing it. It's a road. It starts at the bottom of the map, right at the edge nearest you, and winds up into the hills, past the forest, toward the river. The ink is wet and glistening. You can smell it.

Beside the road, letters form, one by one, in Orrin's handwriting:

*Follow me if you must. But bring the good pen.*

You look round the shop. You look back at the map. The edges of it, you notice now, are wrong. Not torn, not burned. Just *blank*, as if the paper there has forgotten it was ever drawn on, and the blankness is creeping inward, slow as frost on a window.

On the shelf behind the counter, in its velvet case, is the good pen: Orrin's silver nib, the one he uses only for the finest work. Beside it sits his journal, fat and leather-bound and stuffed with loose pages.

## journal
[art: journal | Orrin's journal, full of other people's maps.]
Orrin's journal is a mess. It always has been. He writes in it the way other people talk to themselves: sideways, in the margins, over the top of older notes, with drawings and blots and the occasional pressed flower. You've never been allowed to read it. You sit on his stool with your heart beating far too fast and open it anyway.

Most of it is ordinary. Notes on pigments. Measurements of the river at Tallow Bridge. A rude sketch of the duke. Then, about a month back, the pages change.

*There is a country called the Inkwold,* Orrin has written, pressing so hard the nib has scratched the paper. *It is not on any map of this world, because it IS a map. Every map ever drawn with care becomes a little bit real there. Every road, every river, every town some mapmaker loved enough to draw well. It's where maps go.*

*The Library at Ammerhold keeps them all. Every map in every world, filed and tended. There's always been an Archivist to look after it. Old Sefa held the post for three hundred years. I met her once, when I was your age, apprentice, though I don't suppose you'll believe that.*

*Sefa is dead. I know it because the edges of my maps have started to go blank.*

The next pages are worse. Lists of maps in the shop, with notes: *Coast of Marrow: north edge gone. Tallow town plan: east quarter faded. Old chart of the Shoals: entirely blank, as if it were never drawn.* And then: *It's spreading. When the Inkwold forgets a place, the place begins to forget itself. Out here too. I've seen it. There's a lane behind the tannery that nobody can find anymore. Mrs Hobb's sister lived on it.*

And then, the last entry, written yesterday:

*Somebody has to go to Ammerhold and take up the pen. I'm old, and I've drawn a great many maps, and I'm the only one I know who has been there before. I've built a door. If my apprentice reads this: don't follow. Mind the shop. Sell the duke's map to someone who'll pay. And for heaven's sake, keep the good pen clean.*

Under that, smaller, like an afterthought:

*Rules of the Inkwold, as I remember them. 1. What is drawn there is real, and what is real can be redrawn. 2. Never trust a place that has no name. 3. The Blank is not a thing. It is the absence of a thing. You cannot fight it. You can only fill it.*

You close the journal. You look at the map on the table, at the ink road crawling toward the river, at the white blankness creeping in from the edges.

Orrin Vell is seventy-three years old. He can't see his own hand without his spectacles. And he's gone to a land made of ink, alone, to fight something that isn't even there.

You take the good pen down from its shelf. You put it in your pocket, next to his spectacles, and the journal too.

## follow
[art: crow | Quill, who has opinions.]
You lay your fingertip on the end of the ink road, where it meets the edge of the paper.

It's wet. And cold. Colder than ink should be, cold like a river. You feel it run up your finger and over your wrist like a ribbon, and then the shop tilts, and the window swings past, and the floor drops away beneath you, and you fall *into* the map the way you'd fall into a pond.

You land on your hands and knees on a road.

It's a real road, packed earth and gravel, running away from you up a long green hillside under a pale gold sky. But everything about it is slightly *drawn*. The grass at the roadside has fine, careful lines, like engraving. The clouds have neat crosshatched shadows. When you look at your own hands, the skin has the faintest texture of paper, and your shadow is a flat grey wash.

Behind you, where you came from, there's only a white wall of nothing. Not mist. Not fog. Just *blank*, like the edge of a page.

"Well," says a voice from somewhere above your head, "you took your time."

There's a crow sitting on a signpost by the side of the road. He's very large and very black, with a shine to his feathers like wet ink, and he's looking at you with one bright eye.

The signpost has no words on it. Its arms are blank.

"You talked," you say.

"So did you. Remarkable, isn't it?" The crow ruffles his feathers. "Name's Quill. I belong to the Library. Or I did, when there was someone there to belong to. The old man came through yesterday and asked me to wait for you. He said you'd follow even though he told you not to." Quill tilts his head. "He seemed rather pleased about it, actually, though he tried to hide it."

"Where is he?"

"Gone on ahead, to Ammerhold. He's slow, though. Bad knees. We'll catch him." The crow hops along the signpost. "Have you got the good pen?"

You show him. Quill makes a satisfied sort of croak.

"Walk with me," he says. "It's easier to explain on the move. And we haven't got all day. Nobody has, round here, these days."

You walk. The road climbs the long green hillside, and the crow rides on your shoulder, light as a folded coat. Near the top, you stop and look back, and catch your breath, and not only from the climb.

The whole of the Inkwold lies below you, and it looks exactly like a map. That's the strange thing. Not like a landscape that happens to resemble a map, the way the view from a high place always does a little. It looks like a map someone has laid out on a table and then stood up and walked into. The hills are shaded with careful hatching. The fields are tinted in soft washes, green and gold and brown, with neat dotted lines for hedges. A river winds across the middle of it all, blue and slow, with tiny engraved ripples. Away to the left there's a forest so dark and dense it looks like someone has spilled the ink pot. Far off, a city glitters. And beyond everything, rising out of the haze, a mountain with its head in the clouds.

There are words, too. You didn't expect that. Faint and pale, hanging in the air above the places they belong to, the way names sit on a map: THE WHISPERWOOD, over the dark forest. RIVER LETHE-NOT. VELLUM. AMMER MOUNTAIN. They don't move when you move your head. They're just there, like weather.

"Everyone who ever drew a place with love," says Quill, "drew a little bit of this. Every map of every country there is, the real ones and the made-up ones, if the mapmaker cared enough, a bit of it ends up here. The Wold's made of them. Stitched together. Some of the seams don't line up, so watch your step at the borders."

"And the Library?"

"Keeps them. All the maps, from every world, filed and tended. And the Archivist keeps the Library." He goes quiet for a moment. "Kept."

You look further out, past the city, past the forest, to the very edges of what you can see. And there it is. Not on one side, but all the way round, like the frame on a picture. A thin bright line of white, where the land simply stops. It's further away than the white at the edge of the map on the drawing table, but it's the same white, and it's moving the same way. Inward. You can't see it move, any more than you can see the hand of a clock move. But you know it is.

"How fast?" you ask.

"Faster every day," says Quill. "A month ago you couldn't see it from here at all."

You stand at the top of the hill for a long moment, with the wind tugging at your coat and the crow's claws gripping your shoulder, and you look at the whole of a world made of maps going white at the edges. You think about the maps in the shop, the coastlines and town plans and charts, going blank in their drawers. You think about Orrin, walking this same road yesterday, alone, without his spectacles, squinting at the white and seeing nothing but a blur.

"Come on," you say, and you start down the far side of the hill. Quill makes a pleased noise in his throat and settles his feathers.

"Right. Now pay attention, because I'll say this once. The road splits a mile on. One way goes through the Whisperwood, which is quicker, but trees in the Wold are drawn by whoever drew them, and some mapmakers were not nice people. The other way goes down to the River Lethe-Not, where the ferryman takes fares, but not in coins. Both get you to the crossroads at the bottom of Ammer Mountain." He fixes you with a look. "Which way, apprentice?"

The road splits ahead, just as he said. To the left, a dark mass of trees with neat, inky leaves. To the right, the glint of a river, wide and slow.

## wood
[art: whisperwood | The Whisperwood, drawn by someone with a heavy hand.]
The Whisperwood looks like an engraving from an old book of fairy tales: tall black trunks crosshatched with shadow, leaves drawn one by one with tiny loving strokes, ferns curling at the roots like question marks. The path runs straight into it, narrow and pale.

"Stay on the path," Quill says, settling on your shoulder. His claws prick through your coat. "Whoever drew this wood drew the path last, and very carefully. Paths are safe. Mostly."

Inside, it's dim and green and quiet. Too quiet. There's no birdsong, no wind. Just a soft dry rustling that seems to come from everywhere at once, like pages turning in a library.

You've been walking for a few minutes when you notice that the trees have moved.

You're sure of it. There was a big split oak to your left, and now it's to your right, and there's a birch where it used to be. You stop. You stare at the birch. It doesn't move. You look away, then back. It still hasn't moved. But the oak is gone.

"They move when you're not watching," says Quill. "Don't let it worry you."

"It's worrying me."

"Fair enough."

You walk on, faster. The rustling gets louder. And then, between the trees, off to your right, you see lights: small and golden and floating at about head height, bobbing gently like lanterns. Dozens of them, winding away into the trees along what might be a second, fainter path.

And with the lights comes a sound. Voices. Laughing. Someone is singing a song you half know, a song you think your mother used to sing.

"Don't," says Quill sharply.

But you've already stopped walking.

Ahead on the main path, something is coming toward you too. You hear a rattle and a clank, and then a figure comes round a bend in the path: a short, round woman in a patchwork coat, hung all over with pots and pans and little brass instruments, pushing a handcart piled high with bundles. She stops dead when she sees you.

"Oh, thank the margins," she says. "Another living soul. I thought I'd be the last one through here before it all goes white."

Behind you, the golden lights bob and beckon. The song goes on, sweet and familiar.

## tinker
[art: tinker | Mabbet, who mends things.]
Her name is Mabbet, and she is, she tells you, a tinker. "Mender of pots, sharpener of knives, adjuster of compasses, fixer of whatever's broke. Been walking these roads since your grandmother was a twinkle." She has a face like a cheerful walnut and a voice like a kettle on the boil.

She sits down on her cart and produces a flask of something hot and spicy, and pours you a cup, and pours another into the cap for Quill, who drinks it with great dignity.

"You're headed for Ammerhold," she says. It isn't a question. "Everyone with any sense is headed the other way. It's the Blank, see. It's eating the edges. Whole towns gone. Villages I've mended pots in for fifty years, gone like they were never drawn." She shakes her head. "There's a hamlet called Pellow, out past the Copper Hills. Lovely little place. Best plum cake in the Wold. I went there last month and there was nothing. Not ruins. Not ashes. Just white. Like a page nobody'd ever touched."

"My master's gone to stop it," you say.

Mabbet looks at you over her cup. "The old man with the bad knees? Came through yesterday, asking directions? Bless him. He's brave. I'll say that." She doesn't say what else she thinks. She doesn't have to.

Then she sets down her cup and rummages in her cart. "Here. You'll want this."

It's a compass, small and brass, with a lid that clicks shut. But when you open it, the needle doesn't point north. It doesn't point anywhere. It spins slowly, round and round, as if it's thinking.

"That's a finding compass," Mabbet says. "Doesn't point north. Points to whatever's most *drawn*, if you see what I mean. Whatever's been mapped the most carefully, loved the most. In a place like this, that's as good as true north." She taps it. "When the Blank's close, it'll point dead away from it. Handy, that. And when you get near your old man, it'll point straight at him, because there's no one alive who's been more carefully drawn than a mapmaker who's spent his life drawing."

"I can't pay you."

"Did I ask?" Mabbet sniffs. "Just do me a kindness. When you get to Ammerhold, if there's anything to be done, put Pellow back on the map. With the plum cake."

You promise. She seems satisfied. She hitches up her cart and rattles off down the path behind you, toward the blank edge of the world, singing tunelessly to herself.

The compass needle swings, wobbles, and settles. It points ahead, down the path, through the wood.

"Well," says Quill, "that was a stroke of luck. I'd have got us lost in about ten minutes."

## lights
[art: lantern-hollow | The lights in the hollow.]
"I'll only look," you tell Quill. "I'll keep the path in sight."

Quill says something rude in crow, but he doesn't fly off your shoulder.

You step off the path. The moment you do, the rustling stops. The trees go perfectly still. And the lights brighten, warm and golden, and drift ahead of you through the trees, and the song gets louder.

It's definitely your mother's song. The one about the boat and the moon. You haven't heard it since you were very small.

The lights lead you down into a hollow, a round green dell where the trees stand back in a ring. In the middle of the hollow there's a table, set for a feast. White cloth. Silver candlesticks. Roast chicken, warm bread, a pie with a crisp golden crust, bowls of red berries, a cake with white icing. And around the table sit people, laughing, eating, singing. Not real people, though. People *drawn*: in fine, careful lines, their faces a little too perfect, their colours a little too bright.

One of them looks up and smiles at you. It's a woman with dark hair and kind eyes, and she looks so much like the half-memory you have of your mother that your throat closes.

"Come and sit down," she says. "You must be tired. You've come such a long way."

You take a step forward.

Quill's claws dig into your shoulder, hard enough to hurt. "Look at their feet," he hisses.

You look. Under the table, the people have no feet. Their legs fade away into roots, long and pale and twisting, running down into the earth of the hollow. And around the edges of the dell, in among the trees, there are other figures, not at the table. Standing very still. Grown over with bark. With leaves sprouting from their hair. Travellers who sat down to the feast and never got up.

"Somebody drew this as a trap," Quill says quietly. "Long ago. A nasty mapmaker with a nasty mind. It shows you what you want most and then it keeps you."

The woman at the table is still smiling. "Come and sit," she says again. "You can rest. You never have to go anywhere ever again."

You have the good pen in your pocket. This whole country is made of ink. If this hollow was drawn, it seems to you, then surely it can be redrawn.

## escape-hollow
[art: drawn-door | A door where there wasn't one.]
You pull out the good pen. The nib is already wet, though you never filled it. Of course it is. This is the Inkwold.

You raise it and draw in the air in front of you: a tall rectangle, then a handle, then hinges. Your hand is shaking, and the lines come out wobbly, but they hang there, glowing faintly black against the green of the hollow. You write DOOR across the top in capital letters, because you're not sure how else to make the point.

The woman at the table stops smiling.

"Don't go," she says, and her voice isn't kind anymore. It's thin and hungry. "Don't go, don't go, we've waited so long for someone new..."

The figures around the table are standing up. Their roots are pulling free of the earth with a terrible tearing sound. The lights around the hollow turn from gold to a sickly green.

You grab the handle of your drawn door and pull. It opens. Beyond it there's the path, pale and narrow, with the tall black trees on either side, just as you left it.

You fall through, and slam it shut behind you, and the door folds itself up like a piece of paper and blows away.

You're sitting on the path. Your heart is pounding so hard you can hear it. Quill is on the ground beside you, feathers fluffed out to twice their size.

"Well," he says, after a long moment. "That was a good door. A bit wonky. But a good door."

You laugh, a bit shakily. Then you stop laughing, because you're thinking about the figures grown over with bark, the travellers who sat down and never got up, and the dark-haired woman who looked like your mother.

"Can we do anything for them?" you ask. "The ones who got caught?"

"Not now. Maybe not ever." Quill gives himself a shake. "But if your master really does get to the Library, and if the Library's still working, then maybe someone could come back here with a pen and fix it properly. That's the thing about maps. They can always be redrawn, if someone cares enough."

You get up. Your knees are shaking. But the path is still there, and through the trees ahead you can see daylight, and somewhere beyond that is Ammerhold.

As you walk on, you realise you're humming the song about the boat and the moon. You stop. Then, after a moment, you start again. It's still your mother's song. The hollow doesn't get to keep it.

## redraw-hollow
[art: lantern-hollow | You change the picture.]
You pull out the good pen. The nib is already wet. You don't stop to wonder about that.

You crouch down at the edge of the hollow, where the grass meets the earth, and you begin to draw. Not in the air. On the *ground*, on the hollow itself, as if it were paper, because in the Inkwold it is.

Around the edge of the dell, where the drawn trees stand in their ring, you draw a gate. Then a path leading out of it, a good wide path, with a signpost. You don't write on the signpost what it says; you write what it *means*. You write: HOME, WHEREVER THAT IS.

The woman at the table hisses at you. "Stop that. Stop it at once."

You don't stop. You crawl round to the table, ignoring the hands reaching for you, and you start drawing on the roots. Where the pale twisting roots go down into the earth, you draw feet. Ordinary feet, in ordinary boots. You're not a great artist. The boots are lumpy. One of them has too many laces. But they're *feet*.

And the people at the table begin to stand up.

Not like before. Not hungry. They look down at their new feet with wonder, as if waking up. The too-bright colours fade from their faces, and underneath them are ordinary people: a farmer, an old woman, a girl with a satchel, a man in a merchant's coat. Travellers. Real ones. The feast was the drawing; the people were trapped inside it.

Around the edges of the hollow, the figures grown over with bark are stirring too. Bark cracks and falls away. Leaves drop from their hair.

The dark-haired woman at the head of the table is the last. She isn't real, and you know it. She's part of the trap. But when you look at her, she isn't smiling anymore. She just looks sad.

You draw her a door too. A small one, just her size, in the trunk of the nearest tree. She looks at it, and then at you, and then she steps through it and is gone, and you never find out where it led.

The travellers stumble past you through the gate you drew, blinking, out onto the path, and away through the Whisperwood in every direction, toward their homes, wherever those are. One of them, the girl with the satchel, stops and presses something into your hand before she runs off: a little brass compass, its needle spinning lazily.

"It points at whatever's been drawn most carefully," she says. "My father made it. It's how I found the hollow in the first place. Bad luck, that."

Quill, on your shoulder, makes a small impressed noise.

"That," he says, "was the most reckless thing I have seen a human do in a hundred years."

"Was it good?"

"It was *very* good." He nudges your ear with his beak. "Come on. Let's get out of this wood before it notices what you did."

## river
[art: ferry | The ferryman waits.]
The road to the river winds down through meadows drawn in pale green wash, with tiny careful flowers dotted through the grass like freckles. Everything here is gentle. Even the wind seems to have been drawn with a soft pencil.

The river, when you reach it, is enormous. Far wider than it looked from the hill. Its water is dark and slow and perfectly smooth, and when you look into it, you don't see your reflection. You see pictures. Flickering, moving pictures, a little like memories: a birthday cake with candles, a dog barking at the sea, a hand holding yours.

"The River Lethe-Not," says Quill, from your shoulder. "Flows with everything everyone's ever remembered. Don't drink it. Don't fall in. Very hard to get out."

You walk along the bank toward the jetty, and you notice the people.

They're sitting on the grass at the water's edge, a few yards apart from each other, all the way along the bank as far as you can see. A woman in a travelling cloak. Two old men side by side. A boy about your age with a pack still on his back. They're not talking. They're not moving. They're just sitting with their chins on their knees, looking down into the river, and their faces have the soft, faraway look of someone listening to music in another room.

"Who are they?"

"Travellers," says Quill. "People who came down to cross and stopped to have a look first." His claws tighten on your shoulder. "Don't catch their eye. Not that they'd notice."

You walk past the boy with the pack. Up close, you can see that the grass has grown up round his boots, and there's a spider's web strung between his elbow and his knee, with dew on it. In the water below him, a picture flickers: a kitchen, a big table, a lot of people laughing. He's smiling at it.

"How long has he been there?"

"Since the spring, I think. Maybe last spring." Quill's voice is flat. "The river doesn't hurt anybody. That's what makes it so hard to walk past. It just shows you the best things you've got, and you sit down to look, and you never think to get up."

You make yourself look away from the boy. You make yourself keep walking. Behind you, very faintly, you hear him laugh at something in the water.

There's a jetty at the river's edge, and tied to it, a flat-bottomed ferry with a lantern on a pole. On the ferry sits a tall, thin figure in a long grey coat with a hood. It turns to look at you. Under the hood there's no face, exactly, just a smooth pale surface like the inside of a shell.

"Passage across," says the ferryman. Its voice is soft and dry. "The fare is one memory. Any one you like. I'll know if it's worth the crossing."

You look at Quill. Quill looks back at you. "That's the price," he says. "Always has been. Your master paid it yesterday."

"What did he give?"

"He wouldn't say. He looked sad, though. And then he looked fine. That's how it works. You don't miss it, once it's gone. That's the worst part."

You look along the bank. A little way downstream, past a thicket of reeds, there's a tumbledown boathouse, and pulled up on the shingle next to it is an old raft, made of logs lashed with rope. It looks like it hasn't been used in years. The river is wide and dark and slow.

"Could I cross on that?" you ask.

The ferryman doesn't answer. Quill does. "You could try. People do, sometimes. The river doesn't like it much. It gets... curious. It'll show you things, try to pull you in. But it's been done."

The ferryman waits, patient as stone, its blank face turned toward you.

## pay
[art: memory-river | Crossing the river of memory.]
"All right," you say. "One memory."

The ferryman holds out a long, pale hand. "Think of it," it says. "Hold it in your mind. And then let go."

You think. It's surprisingly hard to choose. Your first day at Orrin's shop, when you knocked over the inkwell and he laughed? No. That one's important. The time you saw the sea for the first time? No. You love that one. Something small. Something you'll never need.

You settle, finally, on a winter morning when you were about seven, and it snowed, and you went out and made a snowman with a carrot nose, and your fingers got so cold they ached. It's a nice memory. But you have lots of winters. You hold it in your mind: the white garden, the cold, the carrot.

And then you let go.

You feel something leave you, like a breath going out. The ferryman's hand closes on nothing.

You try to think of the snowman. There's nothing there. You know there must have been snow, once, when you were small. You know you must have played in it. But when you reach for it, there's just a smooth place, like a stone worn flat by the sea.

You don't feel sad. That's the odd part. You know you should. But there's nothing there to feel sad about.

"Climb aboard," says the ferryman.

The crossing is slow and silent. The ferryman poles the flat boat across the dark water, and the lantern on its pole sways gently, and in the water all around, you see the memories drifting past: thousands, millions of them, flickering like fish. Somewhere in there is a snowman with a carrot nose. You'll never find it.

Halfway across, Quill, who has been very quiet, leans close to your ear.

"He gave a summer," Quill says. "Your master. He told me after, when he'd forgotten why it mattered. A summer when he was young and met a woman called Sefa, at a library on a mountain, and she taught him how to draw a map that was true." The crow is silent for a moment. "He gave away the memory of how he learned to do the thing he's going to do. Can you imagine?"

You think about it. You think about Orrin, seventy-three years old, half-blind, stepping onto this ferry and handing over the summer that made him who he is, so that he could keep going.

The ferry bumps against the far bank. The ferryman says nothing as you climb off. When you look back, it's already pushing out into the dark water, a grey shape fading into the mist.

## raft
[art: raft | The river remembers.]
The raft is older and wetter than it looked. The ropes are slimy with weed, and one of the logs at the end is loose and rolls when you step on it. But it floats. You find a long pole in the boathouse and push off from the shingle, and the current takes you.

At first it's almost peaceful. The river is slow and smooth, and the raft drifts out across it, turning gently. Quill sits on the highest log, watching the water with a very serious expression.

Then the water starts showing you things.

It starts with small ones, flickering just under the surface. A kitten you had when you were tiny, a grey one, chasing a piece of string. The smell of your grandmother's kitchen. A boy you used to play with, whose name you'd forgotten until you saw his face: Tam. It was Tam. The pictures rise up toward you, bright and sharp, and you find yourself leaning over the edge of the raft to see them better.

"Don't," says Quill.

You pull back. But the pictures keep coming, bigger and brighter. Your first day at Orrin's shop, when you knocked the inkwell over and he laughed and laughed. The sea at Marrow, the first time you saw it. ## crossroads
[art: crossroads | The crossroads below Ammer Mountain.]
The crossroads sits at the bottom of a valley, where four roads meet beneath an old stone signpost. Behind it rises Ammer Mountain, huge and grey, drawn in long confident strokes, its peak lost in cloud. Somewhere up there is the Library.

And the Blank is here.

You see it before you understand what you're seeing. To the east, where a road should run off between the hills, there's simply nothing. Not fog, not darkness. White. A clean white edge, like the margin of a page, and beyond it the absence of anything at all. The road runs up to it and stops, as if someone had cut it off with scissors. Half a tree stands at the edge, its branches on one side, nothing on the other.

As you watch, a fence post near the edge flickers, goes faint, and is gone.

"It's moving faster," says Quill quietly. "It wasn't this close yesterday."

You can't stop staring at it. It isn't frightening exactly. It isn't anything. That's the trouble. It's like trying to look at the back of your own head.

You make yourself turn away. On the ground at the foot of the signpost there are footprints in the dust: the long, shuffling prints of an old man with bad knees. And beside them, little drops of black ink. Orrin's pen leaks. It always has.

The signpost has two arms with words still on them. One points up the mountain, along a steep path that zigzags up the rock face in tight switchbacks, carved into steps. It says: THE INK STAIR. ASCENT TO AMMERHOLD, 4 HRS. The other points along the valley floor, toward a glittering spread of towers in the distance. It says: VELLUM, CITY OF GLASS. And underneath, in smaller letters: *Cable car to Ammerhold, departs hourly.*

Orrin's footprints go toward the city.

"Cable car?" you say.

"Vellum's been running it for two hundred years. Lifts you right up to the Library steps." Quill hesitates. "Or it used to. The city's got a lot of people in it right now, all trying to get away from the Blank. Could be crowded. Could be trouble. The Stair's harder, but nobody'll be on it." He clicks his beak. "And there's an old hermit lives halfway up who knew the Archivist. If anyone knows what's waiting at the top, it's him."

The white edge to the east is very quiet. Another fence post is gone.

## city
[art: glass-city | Vellum, the City of Glass.]
Vellum is the most beautiful thing you've ever seen, and it's full of frightened people.

The city is built entirely of glass: towers, bridges, houses, all of it drawn in fine silver lines and filled with pale washes of blue and green and rose. The light passes through it and splits into rainbows that lie across the streets like spilled paint. Glass bells chime in the glass towers. Glass fountains splash.

And everywhere, there are people. Refugees from the edges of the Wold, carrying bundles, pushing carts, holding children by the hand. Villages that have gone white. Farms that have vanished. They're sleeping in doorways and queuing at fountains and gathering in anxious knots on street corners, talking in low voices about the Blank.

"This way," says Quill. "The cable car station's in the main square."

You follow Orrin's ink drops through the streets. They're harder to see on glass, but you find them: a spatter here, a smear there. The old man stopped to rest, you think, on this bench. He leaned on this wall. He was slowing down.

The main square is jammed with people. In the middle of it, at the foot of the tallest tower, stands the cable car station: a glass pavilion with a long cable running up from its roof into the clouds, toward the mountain. A little glass car hangs from it, swaying. There's a queue that winds round the square three times.

And at the gate to the station stands a man in a uniform of silver buttons, holding a ledger.

"No passage to Ammerhold without a written word," he's announcing, over and over. "No passage without a written word. City ordinance. Payment in words only."

"What does he mean?" you ask Quill.

"In Vellum, the currency is words. Written ones. Every word you write here has value. A good one, a rare one, a beautiful one, can buy you a house. The cable car costs a lot. A whole new word, never written before."

You look at the queue. You look at the man with the ledger. You look up at the cable car, dangling against the clouds.

There's a sign on a little shop at the edge of the square, swinging in the breeze: OLD BESS, SCRIVENER. WORDS BOUGHT AND SOLD. And beneath the station, a small open door, half hidden, where a service stair seems to lead up to the cable car's machinery. Nobody's guarding it. Everyone's watching the gate.

## scrivener
[art: word-shop | Old Bess's shop, full of words.]
Old Bess's shop is a narrow glass room lined from floor to ceiling with tiny shelves, and on every shelf there's a slip of paper with a single word written on it. Some of the words glow faintly. Some of them hum. One of them, on a high shelf, is wrapped in a little cage of wire, and it's rattling.

Old Bess herself is tiny and very old, with a pair of glasses so thick they make her eyes look like two blue moons. She peers at you over the counter.

"A word for the cable car," she says, before you can open your mouth. "Everyone wants one. Price has gone through the roof. I can't make new words fast enough." She sighs. "And I'm out. Last one went an hour ago to a funny old man with ink on his fingers."

"Orrin?"

"Didn't give his name. Couldn't see a thing without his specs, poor lamb. I had to read him the menu." She looks at you more sharply. "You're with him?"

"I'm his apprentice. I'm trying to catch up."

Bess considers this. Then she pushes a blank slip of paper across the counter, and a pen. "Then you'll have to make your own. A word nobody's written before. It has to *mean* something, mind. Can't just be a jumble of letters. The gatekeeper'll know."

You take the pen. You stare at the paper. A new word. A word nobody's ever written. Your mind is completely empty.

Quill hops onto the counter. "Think about it the way a mapmaker would," he says. "A map's a word for a place. It's a way of saying *this is here, and it matters.* So what needs saying, that nobody's said?"

You think. You think about the white edge at the crossroads. The fence posts vanishing. The frightened families in the square, carrying everything they own. All the places in the Wold going blank because nobody's there to remember them.

And then you think about Orrin, setting out alone at seventy-three to fix it, without his spectacles.

You write a word. You don't know where it comes from. It just arrives, the way the best lines on a map sometimes do.

*Kepthereness.*

Bess reads it. She reads it again. Then she takes off her thick glasses and wipes her eyes on her sleeve.

"The feeling of a place still being there because someone cared about it," she says. "Oh, that's a good one. That's a very good one." She hands it back. "That'll get you up the mountain. That'll get you anywhere."

The gatekeeper reads your word too, when you get there. He reads it three times. Then he waves you straight through to the front of the queue, and nobody complains.

## stowaway
[art: cable-car | Hanging over the valley.]
The service door leads to a narrow spiral stair, and the stair leads up into the roof of the station, where the great wheels of the cable car mechanism turn slowly in the gloom, glass cogs meshing with glass cogs. It's like being inside a clock.

The cable car itself is docked at a platform at the top. Through its windows you can see the passengers settling into their seats: a family with three children, an elderly couple, a man clutching a birdcage. The gatekeeper's assistant is about to close the door.

There's a ledge running along the outside of the car, just below the windows, where a workman might stand to clean the glass. You look at it. You look at Quill.

"Absolutely not," says Quill.

You climb out onto the ledge just as the car lurches and swings out of the station.

For one long, awful moment you're dangling in space, clinging to a glass ledge with your fingertips, the city of Vellum spread out a dizzy distance beneath your feet. Then you find your footing and flatten yourself against the side of the car, and the car climbs, and the wind howls around you, and Quill clings to your collar with his eyes shut, saying something long and bitter in crow.

The city drops away. The valley drops away. The cable climbs up and up toward the cloud wrapped around the top of Ammer Mountain. You can see the whole of the Inkwold from here, spread out like a map, which of course it is: the green meadows, the dark Whisperwood, the river. And around the edges, everywhere you look, the white. It's much closer than you thought. Much bigger. In some places it's eaten halfway to the city.

A child inside the car sees you through the window and screams. Then a face appears beside the child's: the man with the birdcage, staring at you in astonishment. He pulls down a window.

"Are you *mad*?"

"Probably," you shout. "I'm trying to catch my master. He's gone to the Library to stop the Blank."

The man stares at you for a long moment. Then he reaches out a hand. "Get in, you idiot, before you fall."

Your fingers are slipping on the wet glass. His hand is a foot away. Then two. The car sways, and the wind tears at your coat.

## stair
[art: ink-stair | The Ink Stair.]
The Ink Stair is carved straight into the side of Ammer Mountain, and it is steep. Very steep. The steps are narrow and worn into hollows by centuries of feet, and they zigzag back and forth up the face of the rock with nothing but a long drop on one side.

It's quiet up here. Just the wind and your own breathing, and Quill, who rides on your shoulder and occasionally offers helpful comments like "Mind that one, it wobbles," and "Don't look down, it's very far."

After the first hour your legs burn. After the second, they stop burning and just ache, steadily, like a toothache. The valley falls away beneath you, and you see the crossroads, and the glittering city of Vellum off to one side, and beyond it the dark Whisperwood and the glint of the river. And around it all, at the edges, the white. It's closer than you'd like. In some places it has eaten nearly to the foot of the mountain.

Halfway up, the stair widens into a ledge, and on the ledge there's a hut.

It's a funny little hut, built of stones fitted together without mortar, with a turf roof and a crooked chimney puffing blue smoke. A goat is tethered outside, chewing on a tuft of grass that's been drawn with great care, blade by blade. And in the doorway sits an old man with a long white beard, whittling a stick.

"Ah," he says, without looking up. "The apprentice. Orrin said you'd come."

"Orrin came this way?"

"Orrin came by the city," says the old man. "Took the cable car, the lazy beggar. But he sent a message up the wire for me." He taps his ear. "I hear things, up here. Name's Fennick. Sit down. Have some tea. You're not going to climb the rest of that stair without a rest, not with legs like that."

You sit down. You're too tired to argue. Fennick pours you tea from a dented kettle into a cup with a chip in it. It tastes of smoke and honey.

"I was Sefa's apprentice," Fennick says. "Long time ago. Before your master. Before *his* master. I was meant to be the next Archivist. Spent forty years at that Library learning every map, every road, every river." He turns the stick in his hands. "And then I came out here to make one more map, a map of this mountain, and I never went back."

"Why not?"

Fennick is quiet for a long time.

"Because the Archivist can't leave," he says. "That's the price. You take the pen, you keep the Library, and the Library keeps you. Forever, or near enough. Sefa did three hundred years. I couldn't face it. So I ran away up a mountain and made friends with a goat." He looks at you, and his eyes are very old and very sad. "Your master knows that. He knew it when he went up there. He's going to take the pen and never come home."

> There's no time. Thank him and keep climbing. -> stair-top

## hermit-advice
[art: hermit | Fennick's map of the mountain.]
"There has to be another way," you say.

Fennick looks at you for a long moment. Then he gets up, creaking, and goes into his hut, and comes back with a roll of paper tied with string. He unrolls it across his knees.

It's a map of Ammer Mountain. The most beautiful map you've ever seen: every rock, every ledge, every crack, every twist of the Ink Stair, drawn in lines so fine they seem to shimmer. In one corner there's a little hut with a crooked chimney. Beside it, a goat.

"Forty years I've been drawing this," Fennick says. "One mountain. Every stone of it. Because I thought, if I could draw one thing perfectly, one thing so true that nothing could ever unmake it, then maybe I'd understand what Sefa understood." He traces a line with his finger. "And I worked out something, near the end. Something I never got to tell her."

"What?"

"The Archivist's job isn't to keep the maps. The Library does that by itself. The Archivist's job is to keep *drawing*. To keep adding. New roads, new towns, new rivers. Because a map that nobody's adding to is a map that's already starting to die. That's what the Blank is. Not an enemy. Just what's left when nobody's drawing anymore."

You think about this. "So one person drawing, alone, forever..."

"Can't keep up." Fennick nods. "Never could. Sefa did her best, bless her, for three hundred years. But one pen against the whole edge of the world? It's like trying to bail out the sea with a teacup." He rolls up the map. "It doesn't need one Archivist, apprentice. It never did. It needs everyone. Everyone who ever drew a map and loved the place they drew."

He pushes the rolled map into your hands.

"Take this up to the Library," he says. "Show it to your master. Tell him Fennick says it was never meant to be one person's job. He won't listen, probably. Mapmakers never do. But tell him."

You try to give it back. "It's your life's work."

"It's a map of a mountain," Fennick says. "The mountain will still be here. That's rather the point." He goes back to his whittling. "Now go on. Two more hours to the top, and your knees aren't getting any younger."

You climb. Your legs ache and your lungs burn and the wind tugs at your coat. But the rolled map under your arm feels lighter than paper ought to, as if it's helping, and every time you stumble, the step under your foot seems to be exactly where you need it.

## stair-top
[art: above-clouds | The last steps.]
The last part of the Ink Stair goes up into the cloud.

One moment you can see the whole valley spread out below you; the next there's only white mist, wet and cold, swirling around your face. You keep climbing, one step at a time, your hand on the rock wall beside you. You can't see the drop. That's almost worse.

"Nearly there," Quill keeps saying. "Nearly there. Nearly there." You suspect he has no idea.

Then, quite suddenly, the mist thins, and you climb out of the top of the cloud into blazing sunlight.

The summit of Ammer Mountain is a broad, flat plateau, and all around it, as far as you can see, the cloud lies like a white sea, with other peaks sticking up out of it like islands. The sky above is the deepest, clearest blue you've ever seen. And in the middle of the plateau stands the Library.

You'd expected something grand. It *is* grand: a great round building of pale stone with a dome on top, like an observatory, or a temple. But it's grand the way an old tree is grand, not the way a palace is. Ivy grows up its walls. Its steps are worn. Birds nest in its gutters. It looks like a place that has been loved and lived in for a very long time.

And it's fading.

You can see it as you get closer. The edges of the building are going soft and pale, like a drawing left too long in the sun. One of the pillars by the door is almost transparent. Part of the dome has simply gone, and you can see sky through it.

"Oh," says Quill, very quietly. "Oh, no. It's worse than I thought."

At the foot of the steps, you stop to catch your breath. Your legs are shaking from the climb. Your lungs feel scraped raw. But you made it. You climbed the Ink Stair all the way up, on your own two feet, the old way.

Something odd happens when you look back down at the stair. The steps you climbed, the ones nearest the top, look sharper than the ones below. Brighter. More *there*. As if your climbing them, step by step, had drawn them in a little more firmly.

"Huh," says Quill, who has seen it too. "Would you look at that."

You don't have time to think about it. You climb the Library steps two at a time.

## gate
[art: library-door | The doors of Ammerhold.]
The doors of the Library are enormous: two great slabs of dark wood, carved all over with maps. Coastlines and mountain ranges, rivers and roads, cities and islands, all cut into the wood so finely you could get lost just looking at them. In the middle of each door there's a brass handle in the shape of a compass rose.

They're standing open, just a crack. A drop of black ink glistens on the threshold.

You push through into the Library of Ammerhold.

## library
[art: library-dome | The Archivist's desk, beneath the failing dome.]
Orrin puts on his spectacles, and blinks, and looks at you properly for the first time. Then he looks round at the Library: the fading shelves, the white creeping inward, the hole in the dome. And his face crumples.

"Oh," he says. "Oh, it's so much worse than I could see."

He tells you, in a low, quick voice, while his pen keeps moving across the page. Sefa, the last Archivist, died a month ago, quietly, at her desk, at the age of three hundred and nine. She'd been tired for a long time. And without an Archivist drawing, the Blank began to come in from the edges, eating everything that wasn't being added to, everything nobody was paying attention to anymore.

"Someone has to take the pen," Orrin says. "The Archivist's pen. Sefa's pen. It's here." He nods at a long silver pen lying in a groove at the top of the desk, gleaming. "Whoever takes it up becomes the Archivist. The Library becomes part of them, and they become part of the Library. They can draw faster than any mapmaker alive. Fast enough, maybe, to hold the Blank back. But they can never leave."

"Then why haven't you taken it?"

Orrin looks down at his hands. They're shaking. "I was trying to see if I could do it without," he admits. "I'm a coward, apprentice. I'm seventy-three, and I've been a coward my whole life about this. I came to Ammerhold once when I was young. Sefa offered to teach me. I ran home." He laughs, but it isn't a happy sound. "And now I've come back to do the thing I ran from, and I still can't make myself pick up the pen."

## together
[art: two-pens | Two pens, one map.]
You pull up a stool beside Orrin and open your own pen.

"What are you doing?" he says.

"Helping."

So you draw what you want. You draw the shelves full. You draw the maps on them: of a little lost village famous for its plum cakes; of the Whisperwood and the river and the crossroads; of Tallow, your home, with the shop and the bell over the door. You draw the hollow in the wood with a gate in its side, and a hermit's hut on a mountain with a goat outside, and a city made of glass.

"Two pens," he says slowly. "Two pens can hold it. And four could push it back." He turns to look at you, and his eyes behind his spectacles are bright. "And a hundred could push it all the way back to the edges of the world."

## archivist
[art: silver-pen | The silver pen.]
You step past Orrin and pick up the Archivist's pen.

"No!" Orrin lunges for your arm, but he's too slow, and too old, and too tired. Your fingers close around the silver barrel.

It's warm. And it hums, very faintly, like a bee in a jar. And then the Library *opens*.

That's the only way you'll ever be able to describe it. One moment you're standing at a desk in a round room full of shelves. The next you're *everywhere*: every shelf, every map, every atlas, every road and river and town and coastline drawn in every map in the Library. You can feel all of it at once, the way you can feel your own fingers and toes. You can feel the white creeping in at the edges, cold and numb, like a limb gone to sleep.

And you can *draw*.

The pen moves in your hand faster than you've ever moved anything. Lines pour out of it: shelves, maps, roads, rivers. Where the white was, the colour comes back. The east wing. The north wing. The hole in the dome fills in, stone by stone. The pillars steady. The Library breathes out, a great sigh of relief, and every map on every shelf rustles.

When you finally lift the pen, the Blank has pulled back to the edges of the Wold. Not gone. Never gone. But held.

You look round. Orrin is sitting on the floor beside the desk with his head in his hands.

"You fool," he says. "You brave, ridiculous fool. That was supposed to be *me*."

"You've done enough," you say. And then, because it's true: "You taught me everything. It's my turn."

He looks up at you. He's crying. You've never seen Orrin cry.

"You can't ever leave," he says. "You know that? You'll be here forever. A hundred years, two hundred. You'll never see the shop again, or Tallow, or the sea."

You think about it. It's strange. You know you should be frightened. But with the pen in your hand and the whole Library humming around you, you don't feel trapped. You feel like you've come home to a house you didn't know was yours.

"I can see the shop from here," you tell him. And you can. Somewhere on a shelf near the bottom of the north wing, there's a map of Tallow, and in it is a little shop with a bell over the door. "I can see everything from here."

## blank
[art: the-blank | Facing the edge of the page.]
You turn your back on the desk and walk toward the white.

"Apprentice!" Orrin shouts. "What are you *doing*?"

You don't really know. But something has been growing in you since the crossroads, a feeling more than a thought: the Blank isn't a thing. It's the absence of a thing. And it seems to you that if something is coming toward you, the least you can do is look at it.

You stop at the edge of the white, where the floor of the Library simply ends. It's like standing at the edge of a page. Up close, it isn't frightening. It isn't anything. It's quiet. Very quiet. Clean, like new snow, like fresh paper, like the moment before you draw the first line.

"What do you want?" you ask it.

There's no answer. Of course there isn't. It's the Blank. It doesn't want anything.

But you keep looking. And the longer you look, the more you notice something. The Blank isn't really eating the Library. It isn't hungry. It's just *there*, where things used to be. And where things used to be, there's... room.

You think about the maps in the shop. All the old charts of the world as somebody saw it once, decades ago, centuries ago. Roads that don't go anywhere anymore. Towns that have changed beyond recognition. Coastlines that the sea has moved. Every map is a picture of a moment. And moments end.

"It's not an enemy," you say out loud. "It's a blank page."

"Yes," says Orrin quietly, behind you. "Yes, I suppose it is. But a blank page is still a loss, apprentice. Everything we ever drew, gone."

"Not gone," you say. "Kept. On the shelves." You turn to look at him. "The Library has every map ever made. It doesn't need to be the world as well. The world can just be the world. Changing. Growing. New."

You hold up your pen. The good pen.

"What if we stop fighting it?" you say. "What if we let it come, and then we draw something new on it? Not the old roads. New ones."

Orrin stares at you. Quill stares at you. The Blank, being the Blank, does nothing at all.

Then, very slowly, Orrin begins to laugh.

"Sefa," he says. "She said something like that. That summer. I'd forgotten. I gave it to the ferryman, I think, or I just lost it." He wipes his eyes. "She said the Library wasn't a fortress. It was a seed."

You reach out with your pen, and you touch the white, and you draw the first line.

## flee
[art: slide | Running from the white.]
"Come on," you say. You grab Orrin's arm and pull. "Come *on*. We're going home."

"I can't," he says. "Apprentice, I can't, someone has to..."

"Someone has to get you out of here before you get eaten by a blank page!" You haul him to his feet. He's light, much lighter than you expected, all bones under his coat. "You can't do it, Master Vell. You said yourself. You're too old and too tired and you can't make yourself pick up the pen. So let's go. Let's just go."

Orrin looks at you. Then he looks at the silver pen in its groove, and at the fading shelves, and at the white. Something goes out of him, all at once, like air from a bellows.

"All right," he says, very quietly. "All right."

You run.

Out through the great carved doors, down the worn steps, across the plateau. Quill flies ahead of you, calling directions. Behind you, the Library is fading fast now, without even Orrin's pen to slow it: the dome gone, the walls going, the great doors turning to white paper and blowing away.

"The door," Orrin pants. "My door. It's at the crossroads. Where the road began."

The crossroads is far below, at the foot of the mountain. You'll never get there in time. Unless.

You pull out the good pen, kneel down on the summit of Ammer Mountain, and draw on the rock. You draw a slide. A long, curving, ridiculous slide, all the way down the side of the mountain, through the cloud, down into the valley. It's not a good drawing. It's wobbly and uneven, and one bit has a loop in it you didn't mean to put there. But it's there.

"You can't be serious," says Orrin.

You grab his hand, and you jump.

The slide is fast and terrifying and absolutely wonderful. You hurtle down through the cloud, shrieking, with Orrin clutching your hand and shouting something that might be swearing or might be laughing, and Quill tumbling through the air beside you. You burst out of the cloud into the valley and see the crossroads rushing up to meet you, and the signpost, and beside it, standing upright in the road, a door. A plain wooden door in a frame, with no wall on either side. Orrin's door.

The Blank is close behind you now. You can feel the cold of it, the silence.

You hit the bottom of the slide and roll. Orrin rolls. Quill lands on the signpost with an indignant croak. You scramble up, grab the door handle, and turn to Quill.

"Come with us," you say.

The crow looks at you. Looks at the door. Looks back at the white, rolling across the valley toward the crossroads.

## ending-two-pens
[art: two-pens | The Library of Many Hands.]
[end: Two Pens and a Hundred More]
Orrin's idea is simple. It's so simple that he laughs about it, later, for weeks.

The Library of Ammerhold has always had one Archivist. One person, alone, drawing forever. But nothing in any rule anywhere says it *has* to be one. It's just that nobody ever thought to ask for help.

So you ask.

Quill flies down the mountain and spreads the word. To Vellum, to the refugee camps, to the farms and villages still clinging on at the edges of the white. *Anyone who can draw, come to Ammerhold. Bring a pen.* And they come. Not hundreds at first: dozens. Then hundreds. Mapmakers and scribblers and children with crayons, farmers who've drawn their own fields, sailors who've charted their own coves. Old Bess the scrivener comes, and Fennick down from his mountain with his goat, and a round little tinker called Mabbet who draws a village called Pellow back onto the map, plum cakes and all.

They sit at long tables under the dome and they draw. They draw the places they love. And the Blank pulls back, and back, and back, all the way to the farthest edges of the world, where it sits, quiet and white, like a margin. Like room to grow.

The Archivist's silver pen goes back into its groove. Nobody takes it up. Nobody needs to.

You and Orrin go home at the end of the summer, through the door at the crossroads. The shop is just as you left it, apart from the dust. The map on the drawing table is whole again, every edge filled in. And the lane behind the tannery is back, with Mrs Hobb's sister on it, who has no idea she was ever gone.

But you go back to Ammerhold, every year, for a month. So does Orrin, for as long as his knees allow. You sit at the long tables with the others and draw what you've seen that year: new roads, new houses, a bridge that's been built, a coastline the sea has moved. You teach the children. Orrin teaches you, finally, the things he never let you near: how to draw a map that's true.

On the wall of the shop, above the drawing table, there's a new sign now, painted in Orrin's crabbed hand:

VELL & VELL. MAPS AND CHARTS. EVERY PLACE IS SOMEONE'S.

He says he means it about the business. You both know he means the other thing too.

## ending-archivist
[art: library-dome | The Archivist of Ammerhold.]
[end: The New Archivist]
Orrin argues for a long time. He argues for three days, actually, sitting at the foot of your desk while you draw, telling you all the reasons you can't, shouldn't, mustn't. You let him. You keep drawing. On the fourth day he stops arguing and just sits with you, and on the fifth, he makes you a cup of tea and asks you to show him how the Library feels from the inside.

You try. You don't have the words. But he listens, and he nods, and at the end he says, "Sefa looked like that. When she talked about it. I didn't understand then."

On the sixth day, you send him home.

You draw him a door. A good one, a proper one, with hinges and a brass knocker and a little window at the top, and when he opens it there's the shop, with the bell over the door and the dust on the counter and the late afternoon light slanting through the big window onto the drawing table. He stands in the doorway for a long time.

"I'll come back," he says. "Every month. I'll bring you news. And biscuits. You'll want biscuits."

"I'll want biscuits," you agree.

He goes. The door closes. You go back to the desk.

He keeps his word. Every month, for eleven years, he comes back through the door with news of Tallow and a tin of biscuits. When his knees get too bad for the walk, he sends a girl from the town with the biscuits instead, a sharp-eyed girl with ink on her fingers. He's taken her on as an apprentice. When she's grown, she draws a map of the shop so beautiful that it glows on the Library shelves, and you can visit it any time you like.

You draw. Every day. The Blank stays at the edges, held, and the Inkwold flourishes: roads and rivers and villages, a new bridge in Vellum, a gate in a hollow in the Whisperwood where travellers no longer get trapped. You draw a village called Pellow back onto the map, with plum cakes. You draw a little hut halfway up a mountain with a goat outside, and you make sure the goat is very well drawn.

Quill never leaves your shoulder. He says it's because somebody has to supervise. You know it's not.

And sometimes, on quiet evenings, you take down the map of Tallow from the shelf and look at the little shop with the bell over the door, and you can see the window lit up, and a figure at the drawing table, bent over a map, working late.

It's enough. More than enough. It's home. Both of them are.

## ending-blank
[art: new-road | The first line on a new page.]
[end: The Blank Page]
The first line you draw on the white is a road.

Not an old road, copied from a map on a shelf. A new one. It runs out from the Library steps into the blankness, and as you draw it, the white on either side of it fills with something: grass, at first. Then trees. Then a hill you didn't plan, with a stream running down it that you certainly didn't draw, because you were only drawing the road.

"The world's drawing itself," Orrin whispers. "Look. Look at it."

It is. Once there's a road, there are places for the road to go. Once there are places, there are people to live in them. You draw a line, and the Blank answers with a valley. Orrin draws a bridge, and the Blank answers with a river for it to cross. It's not fighting you. It never was. It was waiting.

Word spreads, the way words do. People come up the mountain to see: refugees from the vanished villages, curious folk from Vellum, an old hermit with a goat. And they draw too. Not to put back what was lost, though some of them do that, and you let them, and it's lovely. Mostly they draw what's new. A village that never existed before. A forest of trees that nobody has ever seen. A city that's half glass and half something else entirely.

The Inkwold is changing. Not ending. Changing.

And in your own world, back through the door at the crossroads, the same thing is happening. Not dramatically. Quietly. The lane behind the tannery comes back, but different: wider, with a bakery on the corner that wasn't there before. A path appears through the hills above Tallow where there was never any path, and it leads to a meadow nobody's ever seen, full of strange blue flowers. Maps in the shop start updating themselves, little by little, as if the world has decided to grow a bit, now that nobody's holding it still.

People are puzzled. Then they get used to it. People get used to most things.

You and Orrin open the shop again, but it's a different sort of shop now. People don't come to buy old maps. They come to report new places, so you can draw them. They come in breathless, saying, "There's a waterfall up past the old mill, there's never been a waterfall up there!" and you get out your pen and say, "Show me."

The Library of Ammerhold has no Archivist anymore. It doesn't need one. It sits on its mountain, collecting every map ever made of every world as it *was*. And the world goes on becoming what it *is*.

Orrin says it's the best thing he's ever been part of. You think so too.

## ending-home
[art: shop-sign | Quill comes home.]
[end: Home Before Supper]
Quill looks at your hand for a long, long moment.

Then he hops off the signpost and onto your arm, and up onto your shoulder, and digs in his claws.

"Fine," he says. "But I'm not sleeping in a cage."

You pull open the door, and the three of you tumble through it into Orrin Vell's map shop, and the door slams shut behind you, and then it isn't a door anymore. It's just a map on a drawing table, pinned at the corners, and as you watch, the last of the ink on it fades away to white.

The Inkwold is gone. All of it. The Whisperwood and the river, the glass city, the Library on its mountain. You'll never know whether the Blank took all of it or whether some of it survived somewhere at the edges. You'll never be able to go back to find out.

For a long time, you and Orrin sit on the floor of the shop and don't say anything.

Over the next few weeks, the world gets a little bit smaller. Not much. Not so most people would notice. But the lane behind the tannery is gone for good, and Mrs Hobb's sister with it, and nobody but Mrs Hobb remembers she was ever there. A village up the coast stops appearing on maps; the post goes there, and the postman comes back puzzled, and then forgets he went. The old charts in the shop's drawers go blank, one after another.

It's hard. You won't pretend it isn't.

But there's something else, too. Something you didn't expect. Orrin, who used to sit at the drawing table all day copying old maps, starts going out. He takes his pens and his paper and he walks the lanes and the fields around Tallow, drawing everything he sees, the way it is now. Not copying. *Recording*. And when you look at his new maps, they're sharp and bright and full of life, and none of them ever go blank.

"Sefa used to say a map was a promise," he tells you one evening. "I gave away the summer she taught me that. But I think I've just learned it again."

You go with him, after that. Every day. You draw the town, the river, the hills, the sea at Marrow. You draw Mrs Hobb's house, and Mrs Hobb, and her cat. You draw the shop with the bell over the door. You get good at it. Really good.

Quill lives on the shop's sign, mostly, where he can watch the street and make remarks about passers-by. He won't talk to anyone but you and Orrin. He says this world is too dull to be worth the effort. But he goes out with you every day when you draw, and sits on your shoulder, and tells you when you've got the shading wrong.

And sometimes, late at night, you take out the good pen and draw a road on a scrap of paper, just a short one, leading off the edge of the page. And you wait, holding your breath, to see if it starts drawing itself.

It never does. But you keep trying. You suspect you always will.

## wait
[art: map-shop | Vell & Apprentice, open for business.]
You put the good pen back in its velvet case. You throw the cloth back over the drawing table. And you turn the sign on the door from CLOSED to OPEN, because Orrin Vell has never once in eleven years let the shop open late, and you are not going to be the one who starts.

He'll be back. He's old, not foolish. Whatever this is, he planned it, and he told you to mind the shop. So you mind it.

The bell rings at a quarter past eight. It's Mrs Hobb from the bakery, with a twist of paper in her hand and a crease between her eyebrows.

"I need a town plan," she says. "The old one, from before the new road. My sister's lane. I went round this morning with a loaf for her, and I couldn't find the turning." She gives a little laugh that isn't really a laugh. "Forty years I've walked to that house. I must be going soft."

You get down the old plan of Tallow from its drawer and unroll it on the counter. You find the bakery. You find the tannery. You find the place behind the tannery where a lane should be.

There's nothing there. Not a gap, not a smudge. The houses on either side sit politely next to each other, as if there had never been room for anything between them.

"Well," says Mrs Hobb, after a while. She rolls the plan up for you, very neatly. "Never mind. I expect I'm muddled." She leaves the loaf on the counter, and goes out, and the bell rings behind her.

At one o'clock you lock up for lunch, the way Orrin always does, and instead of eating you walk down the hill to the tannery.

You've been down the lane behind it a hundred times. Mrs Hobb's sister keeps hens, and sells eggs from a basket on her gate, and Orrin sends you down for half a dozen every Friday. You know the way the way you know your own front door. Past the tannery yard, with its smell. Left at the water trough. Then the lane, narrow and cobbled, with the little houses leaning together over it.

You walk past the tannery yard. You turn left at the water trough.

There's a wall. A plain brick wall, old and mossy, the bricks a little crooked, as if it's been there for a hundred years. On the other side of it, you can see the backs of the houses on Mill Street.

You put your hand flat on the wall. It's warm from the sun, and solid, and real. You stand there for a long time, trying to remember what colour the door of the egg-woman's house was. Green, you think. Or was it blue? You know it was one of them. You *know* you knew.

An old man is coming along with a barrow, and you stop him. "Excuse me. There used to be a lane here. A row of houses. A woman who sold eggs."

He looks at the wall and then at you, kindly, the way you'd look at someone who's had too much sun. "Not in my time, love," he says. "That's always been a wall."

You walk back up the hill to the shop. You don't run. But you walk very fast.

At noon you lift the cloth off the drawing table and look at the map. The ink road has crawled a long way since the morning. It's past the forest now, past a wide dark river, and it has stopped at the foot of a great mountain with a library on top. The white at the edges has crept in further. It's taken a whole corner, hills and all.

You put the cloth back.

At three, the Duke's man comes. He's tall and thin and has never in his life been in a hurry. "His Grace wishes to know," he says, "whether the map of his country is ready to be collected. He is prepared, he says, to discuss payment."

"Payment," you say. "After four years."

"His Grace has had a difficult time," says the Duke's man, without a flicker.

You go to the cabinet where the Duke's map is kept, the beautiful map of a country that doesn't exist, which Orrin drew in a fortnight and called his best work. You take it out. You unroll it.

It's white. All of it. The Duke's invented country, with its rivers and its forests and its palace on a hill, has gone the way of every other place nobody lives in.

The Duke's man looks at the blank sheet for a long moment. "I shall tell His Grace," he says, "that it was very beautiful," and he goes.

After that, you can't stop yourself. You go along the cabinets and open the drawers, one after another, and look.

Most of the maps are still there. The county survey. The river charts. The plans of Tallow, old and new. But here and there, in every cabinet, there's a white one. A chart of a coast that nobody sails to any more. A plan of a manor house that burned down before you were born. A map of a little island off Marrow that Orrin drew when he was young, with a lighthouse and a single cottage on it, and a note in the corner in his young, careful hand: *Visited July. Keeper's wife makes excellent scones.* All of it white, except the note, which hangs there on the blank paper in faded brown ink, as if it's the only thing anyone remembered.

You take that one out and look at it for a long time.

It isn't the important places that are going. It's the small ones. The ones nobody visits, the ones nobody thinks about, the ones only one person ever loved enough to draw. You think about the island with the lighthouse, and the keeper's wife, and her scones, and whether she's still there. Whether she ever was, now. Whether anyone will notice, if she isn't.

Orrin would notice. Orrin noticed everything. That was the whole of his job, he always said: noticing, and then writing it down so other people could notice too.

## night
[art: journal | A lamp left burning.]
You don't go home. You make a bed of coats on the floor behind the counter, and you light the green-shaded lamp on the drawing table, and you sit up on Orrin's stool with the cloth folded back so that you can see the map.

The shop is very quiet at night. You've never been here this late. You can hear the clock in the back room and the river at the bottom of the street and, now and then, a soft dry sound from the cabinets, like a page being turned.

You know what that sound is. You don't open the drawers to check.

At nine o'clock, the ink road on the map stops moving. You watch it for a long time, but it doesn't crawl any further. It ends at the great domed building on the mountain, AMMERHOLD, THE LIBRARY, in a little blot of ink, as if whoever was walking it has sat down.

At ten, you go out into the street for air, and the street sign on the corner, the one that has always said TANNERY ROW, says TANNERY. Just that. You stand under it for a while trying to remember what the second word was for, and then you go back inside, because it frightens you that you're having to try.

At eleven you fall asleep on the stool with your head on your arms.

You dream about the first map Orrin ever let you draw on your own.

You were nine. It was a map of the shop: just the one room, the counter and the cabinets and the globe, nothing difficult. You spent a whole week on it. You measured everything with a bit of string, and drew it out in pencil, and inked it in, and coloured it, and when it was finished you put it on the drawing table for him to see, and stood back with your heart in your mouth.

He looked at it for a long time. Then he said, "Where's the bell?"

You'd forgotten the bell over the door. You'd drawn the door, and the step, and the little window at the top of it, but you'd forgotten the bell. You said it didn't matter, it was only a bell, and he took off his spectacles and cleaned them on his shirt, the way he did when he was going to say something important.

"The bell is how people know they've come in," he said. "It's how *we* know. It's the difference between a room and a shop. You leave out the bell, you've drawn a room." He put his spectacles back on. "A map's not a list of what's there. It's a list of what matters. Put the bell in."

You put the bell in. He kept that map. It's in the bottom drawer of the second cabinet, under the charts of the Shoals. Or it was.

In the dream, you open the drawer to check, and the paper is white, and you wake up with your heart pounding.

At midnight, you wake up, because something is writing.

On the map, beside the Library, letters are forming in Orrin's crabbed hand. They're smaller than before, and shakier, and they keep stopping and starting, as if the hand making them keeps having to rest.

*Still drawing. Hard going. The edges are too far away from here, apprentice. I can't reach them all.*

A pause. Then:

*Mind the shop.*

You sit and look at the words until the ink dries. Then you look at the edges of the map, at the clean white creeping in from all four sides, eating hills and forests and a little village you can just make out by its tiny church spire.

*The edges are too far away from here.*

But they aren't far from you. They're right here, under your hands, on the drawing table in Tallow. You could reach any one of them with a pen.

You think of the thing Orrin used to say a hundred times a year, when he was teaching you to draw: *What is drawn is real, and what is real can be redrawn.* He always meant it as a joke about customers who wanted their maps changed. You aren't sure, tonight, that it was ever a joke.

The good pen is in its velvet case on the shelf. The lamp is lit. The map is waiting.

## feast

## raft-shore
[art: raft | The far bank.]
Then, from a long way off, you hear yourself shout. It's your own voice, but you don't remember deciding to use it. "*Quill!*"

Pain. Sharp, sudden, at the back of your hand. Quill has bitten you, hard enough to draw blood.

You jerk upright. The pictures shatter like a dropped mirror. The river is just a river again: dark, slow, ordinary.

You grab the pole and push, and push, and push, not looking down. The far bank comes closer. Finally the raft runs aground on a shingle beach, and you stagger off it and fall onto the grass and lie there, breathing hard, with the back of your hand stinging and bleeding.

"Sorry about that," Quill says, landing next to your head. He doesn't sound sorry. He sounds relieved.

"Don't be." You sit up. Your hand hurts, but you remember everything. The kitten. Tam. The inkwell. Your mother's song. It's all still there. You didn't give any of it away.

## ferryman
[art: ferry | The ferryman, up close.]
"What do you do with them?" you ask. "The memories."

The ferryman is quiet for so long that you think it isn't going to answer. Then its smooth blank face tilts, very slightly, the way a person's head tilts when they're listening to something far away.

"I let them go," it says. "In the river. That is where they belong. Everything anyone has ever remembered flows here in the end, and goes down to the sea, and the sea keeps it. I only carry them a little way."

"Then why charge for crossing?"

"Because the crossing is heavy," the ferryman says, "and a memory is the only thing light enough to balance it." It lifts its long pale hand and looks at it, as if it hasn't looked at its own hand in a long time. "I was drawn for this. A mapmaker drew a river and needed a way across it, and so she drew a ferry, and a pole, and someone to hold the pole. She drew me carefully. I have been holding it for eleven hundred years."

You look at its hand. Around the edges, very faintly, the fingers have gone pale. Not the pale of skin. The pale of paper.

"You're fading," you say.

"The far bank faded last week," the ferryman says. "A whole village of fishermen who used to cross every market day. I waited at the jetty for them. They did not come. Now there is the old man, who crossed yesterday, and you, and your bird." It turns its blank face back to the water. "I do not mind ending. I mind that there will be no one at the pole when I am gone. People will come down to this jetty wanting to cross, and the river will be wide, and there will be no one."

Quill shifts on your shoulder. "Don't," he mutters. "I know that look. You get it in the shop when someone brings in a torn chart. Don't."

"If I held the pole," you say slowly, "would you last longer?"

The ferryman goes very still.

"If someone else held it," it says, "I could rest. I could sit in the bow and watch the water, as the passengers do. I have never done that." A pause. "But the one who holds the pole does not leave the river. That is how she drew it."

## edge
[art: the-blank | The edge of the world.]
You don't decide to walk toward it. You just find that you're doing it.

"Apprentice," says Quill. "Apprentice, the Library is *that* way."

The eastern road runs out from the crossroads between two low hills, packed earth with grass at the verges, drawn in the same careful engraver's lines as everything else in the Wold. You walk along it. The grass gets sparser. The lines get thinner, as if whoever drew this part was running out of ink. A stone wall beside the road is complete for ten yards, then drawn only in outline, then only as a dotted line, then not at all.

And then you're there.

The Blank, up close, is the quietest thing you have ever stood beside. Quieter than snow. Quieter than the shop at night. The road runs up to it and stops, clean as a cut, and beyond the cut there is nothing. Not white, exactly. White is a colour, and this isn't anything. It's just where the drawing stops.

Half a tree stands at the edge. You walk round it. On one side it has bark and branches and leaves; on the other side, if you put your eye close, you can see the tree's insides, rings and all, like a page cut through the middle.

You hold out your hand.

"*Don't*," says Quill.

The tips of your fingers go into the white. There's no feeling. Not cold, not warm, not wet. There's simply no feeling at all, as if your fingers belong to someone else now, someone you haven't met. And with the no-feeling comes something else. A soft, slow loosening, inside your head. You can't remember the name of the bakery in Tallow. You knew it this morning. It had a blue door. Did it have a blue door?

You pull your hand back. Your fingers are all there. They tingle, the way they do when they've been asleep.

"That," says Quill, and his voice is shaking, "is the stupidest thing I've ever seen you do, and I watched you try to clean the globe with turpentine."

You don't answer. You're looking at the white. It doesn't look like an enemy. It doesn't look hungry, or cruel. It just looks *empty*, the way a page looks before the first line goes down, and you have the good pen in your pocket, and some foolish part of you wants very badly to know what's on the other side of nothing.

Behind you, back at the crossroads, the signpost points up the mountain toward the Ink Stair. Somewhere up there, Orrin is waiting.

## orrin-pen
[art: silver-pen | The pen, in the right hand.]
You pick up the silver pen. It's warm, and it hums faintly against your fingers, like a bee in a jar, and for a moment you feel the whole Library lean toward you, the way a dog leans toward someone it hopes will feed it.

You don't keep it. You turn round and put it into Orrin's hand.

"You're not a coward," you say.

He stares at the pen. His fingers have closed round it by reflex, the way they close round any pen you give him, and you can see them trembling.

"You ran home once," you say. "When you were young. All right. But then you spent fifty years drawing maps so carefully that they're still on the shelves here. I've seen your hand in this Library since we walked in. That harbour chart. That river survey. That town plan with the little bell drawn over the shop door." You point. You can see them, bright and solid in the failing room, the last maps on their shelves still holding out against the white. "You never stopped drawing for the Library, Master Vell. You just did it from the shop."

Orrin looks at the shelves. His mouth opens, and closes.

"And then," you say, "you came back. At seventy-three. With bad knees and no spectacles, on your own, to do the thing you were most afraid of. That isn't what cowards do. I know. I've been one all morning."

For a long moment, nothing happens. The white creeps across the floor. Quill stands on the desk with his feathers fluffed, not saying anything at all, which for Quill is a kind of shout.

Then Orrin Vell takes a breath, and sets his spectacles straight on his nose, and puts the silver pen to the page.

The Library *opens*. You see it happen to him. His face goes still, and then wide, as if he's suddenly standing on a hilltop looking at everything at once. And the pen moves. Not the shaky, frantic scratch of an old man fighting the dark, but long, sure lines that pour across the paper faster than you can follow. Shelves come back. Atlases leap back to their places. The pillar by the door steadies. Overhead, stone by stone, the hole in the dome closes, and the sky goes away.

When he finally lifts the pen, the white has pulled back to the walls. Not gone. Held.

He looks up at you. He looks, you think, about ten years younger, and also about a hundred years older, and you don't know how both can be true.

"Well," he says. His voice is rough. "That's done, then." He clears his throat. "You'll go home now. Somebody has to mind the shop. And I want you to sell the Duke's map to someone who'll pay for it, if it's still there."

## ending-tallow
[art: map-shop | Drawing by lamplight.]
[end: Drawn from Tallow]
You take down the good pen, and you dip it, and you lean over the map until your nose is nearly touching the paper, the way Orrin does, and you start on the nearest edge.

You don't know what was there. That's the first problem. The white has taken it, and there's no copy. So you do what you've seen Orrin do a thousand times with a torn chart: you look at what's left, and you guess what ought to come next. A stream runs toward the edge, so you draw it on, down into a valley. A road runs toward the edge, so you draw it on, past a farm. The farm needs a gate. The gate needs a lane. You draw them.

And the white stops.

Not everywhere. Just where you've drawn. Along the bottom edge of the map, under your pen, the white sits still, like a cat that's been told off, and doesn't come any further.

You keep going. All night. The lamp burns down, and you light another. Your hand cramps, and you shake it out, and you keep going. You draw hills and hedges and a mill with a wheel. You draw a village with a little church spire, the one you saw going white at midnight, as near as you can remember it. You don't know what it was called. You write PELLOW under it, because it seems like the sort of name a village like that would have, and in the morning you'll find out you were right.

Around four o'clock, you notice that some of the lines near the middle of the map are moving on their own. A coastline lengthening. A forest filling in. They're drawn in a hand you know better than your own, shaky but sure, and they're coming toward you from the Library, while yours go toward them from the edges.

Just before dawn, they meet. Your road and his road join in the middle of a meadow, and there's a tiny, deliberate blot of ink where they touch, which you're fairly sure means *well done*.

Orrin comes home a week later, through a door that appears in the wall of the back room where there's never been a door. He's thinner, and very tired, and he has a crow on his shoulder who says, "So *this* is the apprentice," in a tone of deep disappointment, and then refuses to say anything else for a fortnight.

Orrin doesn't say anything at first, either. He walks to the drawing table and looks at the map for a long time: your clumsy edges, your too-wide rivers, your hopeful little village with its guessed-at name.

"They put the old Archivist's pen back in its groove," he says at last. "The Library doesn't need one person drawing from the middle, it turns out. It needs people drawing from everywhere. I worked that out at about four in the morning, when somebody started holding up the edges." He takes off his spectacles and cleans them on his shirt. "That was you, I suppose."

"You said to mind the shop."

"I did." He puts his spectacles back on. "This is not what I meant."

But he takes down a second stool, and sets it at the drawing table beside his own, and from then on there are two of you at it every night. People come to the shop from all over Tallow, and then from further, to learn to draw the edges. The lane behind the tannery comes back. So does Mrs Hobb's sister, who wants to know why everyone keeps hugging her.

## ending-shop
[art: shop-sign | The shop, minded.]
[end: Minding the Shop]
You turn down the lamp. You put the cloth back over the drawing table. You lie down on the coats behind the counter and you don't sleep, but you lie there anyway, because it's what he asked you to do.

In the morning you unlock the door at eight o'clock, and the bell rings.

You mind the shop. You do it every day. You sell charts and settle boundaries and grind pigments, and every evening at six you lock up, and before you go home you lift the cloth and look at the map. The ink road doesn't move again. The white at the edges creeps in a little further each day, and then, one morning in the second week, it stops.

Not slowly. All at once. As if someone, somewhere, has picked up a pen.

After that, the edges begin to come back. A hill at a time. A river at a time. You can see the lines being drawn, faint at first and then firm, in a hand that isn't Orrin's. It's finer than his, and quicker, and it doesn't wobble. Whoever has taken up the pen at Ammerhold, it isn't him.

The lane behind the tannery comes back on a Tuesday. Mrs Hobb runs the whole way to the shop to tell you, in her apron, with flour on her hands.

Orrin doesn't come back.

You wait. You mind the shop. You keep his stool where it is and his pipe dish clean. You make his porridge with salt, the way he likes it, on the mornings you think he might come, and eat it yourself at noon when he doesn't. Customers ask after him. You tell them he's travelling. It isn't a lie. You just don't say where, or how far, or that the road he's travelling on is drawn in ink. In the autumn, a letter arrives, with no stamp, pushed under the door one night. It's in Orrin's handwriting.

*Apprentice. The Library has a new Archivist. A young woman from the city of glass, who draws like water running downhill. She found me half dead at the desk and took the pen out of my hand before I could argue, which is the only way anyone has ever won an argument with me. I'm helping her. I'm teaching her the coasts. I find I am happier than I have been in fifty years, and I'm sorry for it, because it means I'm not coming home.*

*You did as I asked. You minded the shop. I knew you would. It's yours now. The deeds are in the second drawer of the globe cabinet, under the Duke's map.*

*Sell the Duke's map to someone who'll pay.*

You read the letter twice. Then you go to the globe cabinet and open the second drawer, and the deeds are there, and so is the Duke's map, white as snow.

You paint a new sign that winter. VELL & APPRENTICE has always been the name on the door, and you don't change it. You just take down the old board and put up a fresh one, painted in your best lettering, so it'll last.

Sometimes, when the shop is quiet, you lift the cloth on the drawing table and look at the map. The Library on its mountain is whole and bright. And sometimes, right beside it, very small, someone has drawn an old man on a bench in the sun, with his spectacles on.

## ending-feast
[art: lantern-hollow | The long supper.]
[end: The Long Supper]

## ending-river
[art: memory-river | Under the surface.]
[end: What the River Keeps]
You let go.

The water closes over you, and it isn't cold any more. It's warm, and it's full of light. Pictures drift past you like slow fish: a birthday cake, a dog barking at the sea, a hand holding yours. Some are yours. Most aren't. They belong to everyone who ever lived in the Wold, and everyone who ever drew a map of it, and they all came here in the end, and they're all still here.

Your mother is here. She's singing. You find the memory and slip into it the way you'd slip into a warm bath, and you're very small, and you're falling asleep, and she sings the boat song, all of it, every verse. You stay there for what might be a minute or a year.

Then you drift on.

She leans over and taps his page. *The map isn't a copy of the world,* she says. *It's a promise to it.*

You know that young man. His hair is black instead of white, and he can see without spectacles, but you'd know that frown anywhere.

You watch him go home at the end of the summer, frightened of what he's been offered, and you watch the old woman watch him go. She isn't angry. She looks as if she knows he'll be back.

Up above you, on the surface, Quill circles the river for three days, calling your name. Then he flies on to the Library alone.

Orrin is the Archivist of Ammerhold for a hundred and sixty years. He's a good one. Better than he ever thought he'd be. ## ending-scrivener

[end: The Word Shop]

By the end of the month there are a hundred people drawing at Ammerhold. The Blank has gone back to the edges and stayed there.

Orrin writes to you, by cable car. *Who on earth taught all these people to draw?* he asks. *And why does every one of them have a word in their pocket that makes me cry?*

You write back. One word. You made it that morning, and it's the best you've ever done.

He keeps it on the desk, for the rest of his life.

## ending-wings
[art: cable-car | Falling, then not.]
[end: Ink Wings]
Your fingers are slipping. The man is reaching. You aren't going to make it, and you know it, and so instead of reaching back you let go of the ledge with one hand and pull out the good pen.

You fall.

The car swings away above you. The man's face is a white O at the window. Quill is screaming, actual screaming, which you didn't know crows could do. The valley rushes up, fields and roads and the glittering roofs of Vellum, and you're drawing, upside down, at a speed you didn't know your hand could manage, on the only thing you've got: your own coat sleeve.

A feather. Then another. Then a whole row of them, long and black, down the length of your arm. You switch hands and do the other sleeve. They're not good feathers. Quill would have a great deal to say about them. But they catch the air.

You don't fly, exactly. It's more like falling very slowly in a direction you've chosen. You swoop across the valley, way off course, with Quill flapping furiously beside you and shouting instructions, and you come down in a meadow far out toward the edge of the Wold, tumbling over and over in the long grass.

When you sit up, there are people staring at you. A whole village of them. They're standing at the edge of their fields, where the white has eaten everything beyond the hedge, and they've stopped looking at the white to look at you.

"You came down out of the *sky*," says a girl.

"I drew wings," you say, still dizzy. "On my coat."

There's a long silence.

"Can you show us how?" says the girl.

You can't get back to the cable car. It's miles away, and the cloud is closing in around the mountain. So you stay, and you show them. Not wings. Fences, first, to hold the white back from the fields. Then walls. Then a whole new hedge, thick and green, and the white stops at it.

Then you go to the next village. And the next. Quill flies ahead and tells people you're coming. You draw wings on your coat again, better ones, and you fly from place to place along the whole edge of the Wold, teaching anyone who'll learn.

It takes a summer. By the end of it, there are pens moving all along the edge, thousands of them, and the white is going back, foot by foot. When you finally fly up to Ammerhold, landing on the Library steps in a cloud of ink feathers, Orrin is waiting at the top.

"I've been holding the middle," he says. "Somebody's been pushing in from the edges. I wondered who." He squints at your coat. "Those," he says, "are terrible feathers."

"They work."

"They do," he says, and hugs you so hard your spectacles, his spectacles, fall out of your pocket.

## ending-hermit
[art: hermit | Three pens at dawn.]
[end: The Hermit Comes Home]
"Just until morning," you say. "I can't climb another step."

Fennick makes you a bed of goat-hair blankets by the fire, and you're asleep before he's finished telling you to take your boots off.

You wake in the grey before dawn because the goat is screaming.

Fennick is standing in the doorway of the hut. Behind him, past the ledge, the Ink Stair goes down the mountain into the valley, the way you came. Or it did. Now it goes down about thirty steps, and then it stops. Below that, there's only white.

"It came up in the night," Fennick says. His voice is very quiet. "I watched it. The whole bottom half of the mountain."

You grab your boots. Fennick unties the goat. Nobody suggests going down. The three of you, and Quill, climb up instead, as fast as you can, with the white following behind, eating the stair one step at a time.

You come out of the top of the cloud into early sunlight, and there's the Library: ivy on its walls, a hole in its dome, its edges going soft. You go up the steps and through the great carved doors, and Orrin is at the desk under the dome.

He's still drawing. He's been drawing all night. His face is grey, and his hand is shaking so badly that the lines are barely lines. He looks up and squints at you, and he doesn't recognise you until you put his spectacles on his face.

Then he looks past you, at Fennick. And Fennick looks at him. And neither of them says anything for a long moment.

"You're the apprentice who ran away," Orrin says at last.

"And you're the other one," says Fennick.

You look from one to the other, two old men who once sat at this same desk and both went home. Then Fennick walks across the room to the groove at the top of the desk, where the silver pen lies.

"I was meant to take this," he says. "Sefa trained me for forty years. I ran away up a mountain because I was afraid of never leaving. And I've spent the next forty years drawing one mountain, and do you know what I found out? I never left that, either." He picks up the pen. "Might as well not leave somewhere useful."

The Library opens around him. You see it. And the old hermit draws, and the white stops, and then goes back, all the way down the mountain and across the valley, stair by stair.

Afterwards, Orrin sits on the floor by the desk with his head in his hands, laughing, or crying, you can't tell. Fennick keeps drawing. The goat eats an atlas.

You and Orrin go home a month later, when he's rested. Fennick stays. He draws the hut on the mountain into the Library's own walls, so he can keep it, and he keeps the goat, and when you visit him, every spring, he's always in the middle of telling you something important, and he never quite finishes.

## ending-edge
[art: the-blank | A light at the edge.]
[end: Off the Edge of the Map]
You step into the white.

There's no sound. There's no feeling. There's no up and no down and no ground under your feet, though you're standing on something. Quill's voice cuts off behind you as if someone has closed a door.

And the forgetting starts.

It's gentle. It isn't frightening. It's like the moment of falling asleep, when the day starts to come apart in your hands. The bakery's blue door goes. The name of your street. The way to the shop. Things you didn't know you knew slip away like water through your fingers, and you don't even feel them go, only the space where they were.

You hold onto one thing. You don't know why that one. A pair of spectacles in your pocket, wire-rimmed, with string tied round one arm. Somebody needs these. You don't remember who. But somebody needs them, and you've got them, and you can't put them down.

You also have a pen.

You take it out. You don't remember where it came from either. But your hand knows what to do with it, the way your feet know how to walk. And in the white, in the nothing, you draw a lamp. A tall iron lamp post, the kind that stands on the corner of a street, with a lantern on top. It's the first thing that comes into your head.

It lights.

You stand under it for a long time. The light makes a small circle round your feet, and inside the circle there's ground, and grass, and a short stretch of cobbled road. Outside it, the white. Inside it, you can remember things again. Slowly. A shop. A bell. An old man's face, squinting at you, smiling.

You never find your way back. You never really try. Something happens to you in the white that you can't explain afterwards, and anyway there's no one to explain it to.

But years later, when the Blank has been pushed back to the edges of the Wold by a whole Library full of people drawing, travellers who walk east from the crossroads find a lamp post right at the edge of the white. It's always lit. Under it, there's a little circle of cobbles and grass and a bench, and a pair of wire-rimmed spectacles, folded neatly, with string tied round one arm.

And in the white beyond the lamp, if you look very carefully, there's a road. A thin one, drawn in a careful, patient hand. It goes out into the nothing a little further each year.

The old Archivist comes down from the mountain to see it, once, leaning hard on a stick. He sits on the bench under the lamp for a whole afternoon, holding the spectacles, looking out at the road.

"Still drawing," he says, to no one, at the end. "Good."

He leaves a tin of biscuits on the bench. In the morning it's gone.

## ending-assistant
[art: library-dome | Tea under the dome.]
[end: The Archivist's Tea]
"Don't go," you say.

Orrin looks up at you from the floor.

"You keep saying I'll never see the shop again," you say. "But I can. It's right there on a shelf. And you keep saying I'll be alone here, and maybe I will be. But I don't have to be. Not straight away." You hold out the hand that isn't holding the silver pen. "Stay a while. Teach me. You never let me near the hard parts. Coastlines. Mountain passes. I don't know how to draw the sea."

"I'm seventy-three," Orrin says. "My knees are gone. My eyes are gone. I'd be no use to you at all."

"You'd make the tea."

He laughs, despite himself. It comes out wet. Then he takes your hand and lets you pull him up.

He stays.

He means it to be a month. Then a year. The Library gives him a little room off the north wing, with a window that looks out over the clouds, and a bed that's too soft, which he complains about every morning. He makes the tea. He sits beside you at the desk with his spectacles on the end of his nose, and he teaches you the sea. How it's never one line but a hundred, all of them moving. How a harbour should look like it's holding something. How every coast is a conversation between the land and the water, and you have to draw both sides of it.

You get good. Really good. Some days, he says, you're better than he ever was.

He doesn't go home. You both know he's not going to, by the end of the first winter, and neither of you says so. He's not an Archivist; he's not bound to the place. He could leave any day. He just doesn't.

He gets older, the way you don't any more. Slowly, and then not so slowly. His hand gets shakier and his eyes get worse, and in the end he can't draw at all, only sit beside you and watch. You draw him things while he watches. The shop. The sea at Marrow. A village called Pellow, with plum cakes. You put them on the shelves where he can see them.

On the last evening, he asks you to draw the summer he gave away to the ferryman. You don't know what it looked like. But you've heard enough, over the years, to guess. A young man at this same desk. An old woman beside him, leaning over to tap his page.

You draw it. He looks at it for a long time.

"That's right," he says. "That's exactly right. How did you know?"

You don't, really. But you're the Archivist of Ammerhold, and the Library holds everything ever drawn with care, and some things, it seems, come back if you draw them carefully enough.

Quill sits on the end of his bed all night. In the morning, you make the tea yourself. You make two cups, out of habit, and you drink them both.

## ending-margin
[art: new-road | Unfolding the white.]
[end: The Folded Places]
You don't draw the first line. Instead, you put the good pen in your pocket, and you step forward into the white.

"*No!*" Orrin shouts, behind you, and then his voice is gone.

Inside, it's quiet. Not empty. You'd expected empty, but it isn't. The white isn't nothing, once you're in it. It's more like a page that has been folded over and over on itself, so many times that you can't see what's printed on it. You can feel the folds, though. They're everywhere. Thin and soft and layered, like a great book with all its pages stuck together.

You reach out, very carefully, and pick at the corner of one.

It comes up. It unfolds in your hands, crackling, and there's a lane behind a tannery, with a row of little houses on it, and a woman in one of the doorways looking up at the sky with a puzzled expression. She's just standing there. She doesn't see you. She's been standing there, you think, since the moment the lane was folded away.

You smooth it flat. It stays.

They're not gone. That's the thing. None of it was ever gone. The Blank doesn't eat the places nobody's paying attention to. It folds them up and puts them away, like a tidy person putting away clothes nobody wears. It's not cruel. It's housekeeping.

You unfold another. A village with a church spire and a bakery with a blue door, smelling of plums. Another: a whole fishing town on the bank of a wide dark river, where the boats are just setting out. Another: a beautiful palace on a hill, in a country with silver rivers, that somebody once drew for a duke who never paid.

You keep going. You lose track of time. You unfold forests and hamlets and lanes and coves, one after another, and smooth each one flat, and they stay. When you finally come out of the white again, blinking, into the Library, you've been gone for nine days, and you have a whole country spread out behind you like washing on a line.

Orrin is sitting at the desk. He hasn't slept. He has, however, taken up the silver pen at last, somewhere around the second day, out of sheer worry, and he's been drawing ever since, holding the middle while you were away.

He looks up at you. You look at him. He opens his mouth, and you know he's going to shout.

"I found Pellow," you say, before he can. "And the Duke's country. And the lane. They were folded up."

Orrin closes his mouth. Then, very slowly, he puts down the silver pen and holds out both arms, and you go into them.

After that, it's simple. Orrin holds the middle, at the desk, and you go into the white, once a week, to see what's been put away that oughtn't to be. Most of it you bring back. Some of it you leave, because some things are folded away for good reasons. The Library is the only place in any world that has its own unfolder, and you have business cards printed, which annoys Quill enormously.

## ending-crow
[art: crow | Quill stays.]
[end: The Crow Archivist]
You start to hold out your hand. Then you stop.

Quill has turned his head. He's not looking at you. He's looking at the mountain behind the crossroads, at the cloud wrapped round its peak, at the Library somewhere up there going white.

"I belong to the Library," he says quietly. "I told you that. The first thing I ever told you."

"Quill..."

"Go on." He fluffs his feathers. "Take the old man home. Somebody has to mind the shop."

You want to argue. You don't have time. The white is rolling down the valley, and Orrin is pulling at your sleeve, and so you pull open the door, and you and Orrin fall through it into the shop, and the door slams behind you and turns back into a map on a drawing table.

Then you sit on the floor and you cry, because you think he's gone.

For a week, the map on the drawing table stays white. The world shrinks a little, the way it does when nobody's looking after the edges. The lane behind the tannery doesn't come back. Orrin sits at the table every day staring at the blank sheet, and doesn't draw.

On the eighth day, there's a mark on the map.

It's in the very middle. It's tiny. It's very bad. It looks like somebody has dipped a twig in ink and dragged it across the paper. It's a line, sort of. It might be a wall.

The next day, there's another one. And another. A shape like a dome, very lopsided. A thing that might be a door, or might be a sheep. And then, on the tenth day, letters. Big, scratchy, sprawling letters, the kind of letters a crow might make if a crow held a silver pen in his beak and had been watching an apprentice draw for a whole day and a half, and had a great deal of determination and very little else.

*NOT DEAD. LIBRARY NOT DEAD EITHER. DRAWING IS HARD. HOW DO YOU DO TREES.*

Orrin makes a sound you've never heard him make. Then he grabs the good pen, and a sheet of paper, and he begins to draw, very carefully, very slowly, the way he'd draw for a child: *a tree*. A trunk. Branches. Leaves, one at a time. He writes LIKE THIS underneath it. And he pins it to the map, right in the corner, where a crow at a desk on a mountain might be able to see it.

The next morning there's a tree on the map, beside the Library. It's terrible. But it's a tree.

It takes years. The Inkwold comes back slowly, in the strangest, scratchiest, most crow-like lines anyone has ever seen, and you and Orrin send drawings through the map every day to show him how. The lane behind the tannery comes back a little crooked. Pellow comes back with its church spire on upside down for the first six months. Nobody minds.

Quill is a terrible artist. He's also, everybody agrees, an excellent Archivist. He's bossy, and he's thorough, and he's never once complained that the job lasts forever.

## ending-tallow-library
[art: library-door | The Library, on a door.]
[end: The Library on Tallow Street]
"Wait," you say.

Quill is on your arm. Orrin is in the doorway. The white is close enough now that you can feel its quiet on the back of your neck. And you're looking at the door. Orrin's door. A plain wooden door in a frame, with nothing on either side.

The Library's going. All of it. Every map in every world, all those shelves, and nobody to keep them. But a door is a thing you can draw on. And it's going through with you.

You pull out the good pen and you draw. Fast. Not well. You draw the Library on the door: a dome, a ring of shelves, the great round desk, as much of it as you can get onto one panel of wood. You draw the silver pen lying in its groove. You draw the carved doors with their compass-rose handles. You draw a crow on the dome, because it seems right.

"Apprentice," Orrin says. "Apprentice, *now*."

You go through.

On the other side, it's the shop, and the door slams behind you, and turns back into a map on the drawing table, and the map goes white. All of it. Every line. The Inkwold is gone.

Except for one thing. Lying on the floor of the shop, where it fell off the door when you tumbled through, is a panel of wood about the size of a tea tray. And on it, drawn in wobbly, hurried lines, is a library.

Over the next few weeks, it grows.

It starts small. A shelf appears in the back room, where there wasn't a shelf. It has maps on it you've never seen. Then another. The back room gets bigger, somehow, without the outside of the shop changing at all. A spiral staircase appears in the corner, going up into a ceiling that's suddenly much higher than it used to be. A great round desk turns up one morning under the skylight, with a silver pen lying in a groove at the top.

Orrin stands and looks at it for a long time.

"Well," he says, at last. "I suppose it had to go somewhere."

The Library of Ammerhold is on Tallow Street now. It's in the back of a map shop, behind a counter, through a door with a bell over it. It's much bigger on the inside. Quill lives in the rafters and is extremely rude to customers.

And people come. Mapmakers, at first. Then anyone. They come in off the street and sit down at the desk and draw what they love, and the maps go onto the shelves, and somewhere far away, where a country of ink used to be, a country of ink begins again, drawn this time by the whole town of Tallow.

The silver pen stays in its groove. Nobody takes it up. Nobody needs to.

On the door of the shop there's a new sign. Orrin painted it himself. It says: VELL & APPRENTICE. MAPS, CHARTS, AND LIBRARY. PLEASE WIPE YOUR FEET.

## ending-old-archivist
[art: library-dome | The Archivist of Ammerhold.]
[end: The Old Man's Pen]
You argue, of course. You argue for an hour. But Orrin has the silver pen in his hand and the whole of the Library humming round him, and he's never been easy to argue with, and now he's impossible.

"I've spent fifty years minding the wrong thing," he says. "Let me mind the right one. And you mind the shop. That's how it was always supposed to go, I think. I was just too much of a coward to see it."

He draws you a door. It's the best door you've ever seen: oak, with a brass knocker in the shape of a compass rose, and a little window at the top, and through the window, the shop. Your shop. With the dust on the counter and the late sun on the drawing table.

"Will you come and visit?" you ask.

"Archivists can't leave."

"Will you write?"

He smiles. "Look at the map," he says. "Every day. I'll be on it."

You go home. Quill stays; the Library is his, and he's Orrin's now too. He walks you to the door, which for a crow is a long hop, and he says, "Don't sell the globe. I like the globe," and then flies back to the desk.

You mind the shop.

And Orrin keeps his word. Every morning, when you lift the cloth on the drawing table, the map has changed overnight. New roads. New rivers. A bridge in the glass city. Little notes in the margins, in his crabbed hand: *Fixed the hollow in the Whisperwood. Gate faces east. Travellers getting out nicely.* *Drew Pellow back. Plum cake looks a bit flat, will try again.* *Fennick's goat has eaten an atlas. Fennick says this is my fault.*

Sometimes, the notes are for you. *Your left-hand coastlines lean. Fix it.* *Heard the Duke's man came round. Do not give him the map for half price. I know you.* *Mrs Hobb's sister's lane is back. Go and see.*

You go and see. It's back.

You get older. He doesn't, much. That's the way of it. You take on an apprentice, eventually, a sharp-eyed boy who knocks over the inkwell on his first day, and you laugh until you have to hold onto the counter. You show him the map. You show him the notes. You tell him that the Archivist of Ammerhold was your master, once, and he doesn't believe you.

That evening, in the margin of the map, very small, there's a drawing of a boy knocking over an inkwell, and an old man laughing.

## ending-ammerhold
[art: two-pens | Master and apprentice, under the dome.]
[end: Apprentice of Ammerhold]
"No," you say.

Orrin blinks at you.

"You'll need an apprentice," you say. "Sefa had one. Two, actually, and they both ran away. You need somebody who won't."

"You can't stay. You've got a whole life..."

"I'm not taking the pen," you say. "I can leave whenever I like. I just don't like."

He looks at you for a very long time. Then he sits back down at the desk, very slowly, as if his knees have finally given up arguing with him, and pulls up a second stool.

"The east wing, then," he says. "Shelves forty to sixty. The Archivist can't be everywhere at once, it turns out. Let's see what you can do."

You can do a lot, as it happens. More than either of you expected.

You stay at Ammerhold for nine years. Orrin draws from the desk, with the silver pen, and the Library breathes through him; and you go out, with the good pen, to the places the Library can't quite reach. The Whisperwood, where you redraw the hollow so that nobody gets trapped there again. The River Lethe-Not, where you build a bridge, which the ferryman says is a liberty. The glass city. The edges. You learn the coasts at last. You learn the sea.

Every evening you come home to the Library and tell Orrin what you've seen, and he draws it in, and it stays.

In the tenth year, he puts down the silver pen one evening and looks at you over his spectacles, the way he used to look at you in the shop when you'd done something right and he didn't want to make a fuss.

"You could take it," he says. "Whenever you're ready. You'd be better at it than me."

"Would you go home?"

"I'd go home. I'd mind the shop. I'd sit in the sun." He smiles. "I'd have biscuits. I haven't had a biscuit in nine years."

You think about it. You think about it for a long time, as long as you've ever thought about anything.

"Not yet," you say. "Ask me again in another nine."

He laughs, and picks the pen back up, and goes on drawing. Outside, the Wold is the brightest it's been in a thousand years, full of roads, full of people. Somewhere in it, a crow is making rude remarks to a goat. And at the desk under the dome, there are two of you, and there's always tea.

## ending-two-archivists
[art: two-pens | Two hands on one pen.]
[end: Two at the Desk]
"No," you say. "Not a hundred. Not yet."

Orrin looks at you, surprised.

"If we call everyone up the mountain," you say, "it'll take weeks. The Blank's still coming. And I don't..." You stop. You look at the silver pen in its groove. "I don't want you to do it alone. That's all. And I don't want to do it alone either."

Orrin follows your eyes to the silver pen. You can see him understand.

"Nobody's ever tried it," he says slowly. "Two hands."

"Nobody ever tried two pens, either. Until about an hour ago."

He laughs. Then he stops laughing, and he looks at you properly over his spectacles, and you see him weighing it, the way he weighs a coastline before he draws it. Everything it'll cost. Forever, or near enough. The shop, the sea at Marrow, the sun on the bench outside the bakery. All of it, for both of you.

"You're sure," he says.

You're not. But you nod.

He reaches out and picks up the silver pen. Then he holds it out, and you put your hand over his, and you hold it together.

The Library opens. Not to one of you. To both. You feel it the way you'd feel a great house filling up with light, room after room, and you feel Orrin in it too, right beside you, the shape of his attention like a hand on your shoulder. You can feel what he's thinking, almost. Not words. Just the way he looks at things: carefully, lovingly, a little grumpy.

And you draw. Both hands on one pen. It shouldn't work, and it does. His precision and your speed. His coastlines and your roads. The Blank goes back so fast you can hear it, like a tide going out.

You stay. Both of you. A hundred years and more. You're the first Archivists of Ammerhold who ever shared the post, and the Library is different for it: warmer, noisier, full of arguments about whether a river should go left or right. Quill says it's like living with two cats in one basket. He doesn't leave either.

You never go back to the shop. But you draw it, once a year, on the anniversary of the morning the bell didn't ring: the counter, the cabinets, the globe in its brass cradle, the bell over the door. You draw it together. And on the shelf in the north wing where its map is kept, if you take it down, you can see a light in the window, and two stools at the drawing table, side by side.
